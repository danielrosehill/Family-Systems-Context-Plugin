# Frameworks: the research this workspace is built on

This file is the evidence base. Every role, pattern or dynamic recorded in this
workspace should name the concept it uses from here, and carry that concept's
**evidence tier**, so a reader can tell a measured finding from a useful metaphor.

Agents: read this before `analyse-dynamics`, and whenever you are about to name a
pattern. Do not introduce a concept that is not here without saying where it comes from
and what tier it is.

## Evidence tiers

| Tier | Meaning | How to use it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A** | Empirically studied construct with validated measures and replicated findings | May be stated as a pattern the literature recognises; still not a diagnosis of anyone |
| **B** | Established clinical theory, taught and used in family therapy, partial empirical support | Use as a lens: "seen through Bowen's concept of triangles, ..." |
| **C** | Heuristic or popular framework; useful vocabulary, weak or no validation | Use only as the user's own language or as a metaphor, labelled as such |

A tier describes the **concept**, not the claim. A tier-A concept applied to thin evidence
is still a weak claim. Strength of a specific claim comes from the instances linked to it
(see `method.md`, "Layers of a claim").

## Rules that come before any framework

1. **The user's account is the baseline.** The user lived in this system; their account
   is recorded as the account, not as one side to be weighed. The agent does not
   second-guess it, ask for proof, offer the other person's perspective, or soften what
   happened. Tiers and layers in this workspace are about **precision**, so a therapist or
   another tool can read the map, never about whether the user is believed.
2. **The user's words are kept, and mapped.** Terms from the informal abuse-recovery
   literature ("flying monkeys", "hoovering", "golden child") are recorded exactly as the
   user uses them and are never discouraged or corrected. [`glossary.md`](glossary.md)
   maps each to what the user means and to the nearest research construct, so the agent
   understands it and a clinician can read it in their own vocabulary.
3. **The workspace records; it does not diagnose.** The agent describes what people did and
   the effect, and does not itself assign clinical diagnoses. When the user calls someone
   a narcissist, that is recorded as the user's characterisation, in their words, and it
   stands. A diagnosis made by a clinician is recorded as such, with its source. (Clinicians
   follow the same line: they do not diagnose people they have not examined; American
   Psychiatric Association, *Principles of Medical Ethics*, Section 7.3.)
4. **The user is in the system too.** Every systemic school below treats the person
   describing the family as part of it, so the workspace keeps an actor file for the user
   (`actors/self.md`). This is never blame. It records the roles the user was put in, the
   survival strategies they developed, and the changes they are making.

## Family systems theory (Bowen) — Tier B

Murray Bowen's theory treats the family as an emotional unit in which members' functioning
is interdependent. Sources: Bowen, M. (1978). *Family Therapy in Clinical Practice*. Jason
Aronson. Kerr, M. E., & Bowen, M. (1988). *Family Evaluation*. Norton.

| Concept | What to look for |
| --- | --- |
| **Differentiation of self** | Ability to hold one's own position while staying in emotional contact. Low differentiation: fusion (thinking and feeling as the family does) or reactive distance |
| **Chronic anxiety** | Background tension the system manages through the mechanisms below |
| **Triangles** | Two people manage tension between them by pulling in a third (a child, a sibling, an in-law, an affair, an illness). The basic unit of a stable system |
| **Nuclear family emotional process** | Four patterns: marital conflict; dysfunction in one spouse (over- and under-functioning); impairment of a child (projection); emotional distance |
| **Family projection process** | Parents' anxiety focused onto one child, who becomes the identified problem |
| **Multigenerational transmission** | Patterns and levels of differentiation repeating across generations |
| **Emotional cutoff** | In Bowen's theory, reducing or ending contact as a way of managing fusion. This is a description from the theory, not a judgement on the user's choices: low or no contact with an abusive person is the user's decision, recorded as such and never questioned. The concept is most useful for patterns *between other members* (a parent cut off from their own family of origin) |
| **Sibling position** | Birth order and its expected functioning (the weakest-supported Bowen concept) |

Empirical note: differentiation of self has validated measures, the Differentiation of
Self Inventory (Skowron, E. A., & Friedlander, M. L. (1998). *Journal of Counseling
Psychology, 45*(3), 235–246) and its revision (Skowron & Schmitt, 2003). Many other
Bowen concepts are less directly tested.

## Structural family therapy (Minuchin) — Tier B

Salvador Minuchin described families by their organisation. Source: Minuchin, S. (1974).
*Families and Family Therapy*. Harvard University Press.

| Concept | What to look for |
| --- | --- |
| **Subsystems** | Parental, spousal, sibling, and any others (grandparent + child) |
| **Boundaries** | Clear (healthy), **enmeshed** (diffuse; no privacy, intrusion, mind-reading, guilt for separateness) or **disengaged** (rigid; little support or responsiveness) |
| **Hierarchy** | Who holds authority. Inverted when a child carries parental functions |
| **Coalitions** | Two members aligned against a third. **Cross-generational coalition**: a parent and a child against the other parent |
| **Triangulation** | Each parent demands the child's loyalty against the other |
| **Detouring** | Parents unite by focusing on a child as the problem (attacking or protecting) |

## Genogram conventions — Tier B (standard clinical tool)

Use genogram relationship types for every pair in `family-overview.md`. Source:
McGoldrick, M., Gerson, R., & Petry, S. (2020). *Genograms: Assessment and Treatment*
(4th ed.). Norton.

Relationship types used in this workspace: `close`, `very-close`, `fused`, `distant`,
`conflictual`, `fused-conflictual`, `hostile`, `abusive` (physical, emotional, sexual,
financial; name which), `cutoff`, `estranged`, `unknown`.

## Contextual therapy (Boszormenyi-Nagy) — Tier B/C

Focus on loyalty and fairness across generations. Source: Boszormenyi-Nagy, I., & Spark,
G. M. (1973). *Invisible Loyalties*. Harper & Row.

- **Invisible loyalties**: unspoken obligations to the family that shape choices.
- **Ledger of merits**: who has given and who is owed. Imbalances persist across generations.
- **Destructive entitlement**: a person deprived of fair treatment as a child feels entitled
  to take from others, often their own children.
- **Split loyalty**: a child forced to choose between parents.

## Parentification — Tier A for the association, B for the typology

A child takes on adult functions for a parent or the family: **instrumental** (care,
household, finances, siblings) or **emotional** (confidant, mediator, the parent's
emotional regulator). Sources: Boszormenyi-Nagy & Spark (1973); Jurkovic, G. J. (1997).
*Lost Childhoods: The Plight of the Parentified Child*. Brunner/Mazel.

Evidence: a meta-analysis found a reliable but small association between self-reported
childhood parentification and adult psychopathology, moderated by sample, measure and type
of outcome (Hooper, L. M., DeCoster, J., White, N., & Voltz, M. L. (2011). *Journal of
Clinical Psychology, 67*(10), 1028–1043). Record it as a real pattern; do not use it to
predict or explain the user's whole history.

## Scapegoating and the identified patient — Tier B

One member is cast as the problem, which stabilises the rest. Source: Vogel, E. F., &
Bell, N. W. (1960). The emotionally disturbed child as the family scapegoat. In N. W.
Bell & E. F. Vogel (Eds.), *A Modern Introduction to the Family*. Free Press. Closely
related to Bowen's family projection process and Minuchin's detouring.

## Family roles in addicted and dysfunctional families — Tier C

The roles hero, scapegoat, lost child, mascot and enabler (Wegscheider-Cruse, S. (1981).
*Another Chance*. Science and Behavior Books; Black, C. (1981). *It Will Never Happen to
Me*) are widely used in recovery and self-help literature, along with the popular
"golden child" / "scapegoat" pairing.

Evidence: a review of the research found mixed support, little attention to diverse
families, and questioned whether the classification's clinical value outweighs its
problems (Vernig, P. M. (2011). Family roles in homes with alcohol-dependent parents: An
evidence-based review. *Substance Use & Misuse, 46*(4), 535–542.
https://doi.org/10.3109/10826084.2010.501676).

How to use: these names are often how users already understand their family, and they are
valid as the user's vocabulary. Record them as `role: scapegoat (user's term; Tier C)`, and
add the *function* (who absorbs blame, who carries the family's image, who
disappears) so the record still means something without the label. Roles shift; record
when and why.

## Communication stances (Satir) — Tier C

Under stress, people adopt **placating**, **blaming**, **super-reasonable** (computing) or
**irrelevant** (distracting) stances instead of **congruent** communication. Source: Satir,
V. (1972). *Peoplemaking*. Science and Behavior Books.

## Drama triangle (Karpman) — Tier C

Persecutor, rescuer and victim positions that people rotate through. Source: Karpman, S.
(1968). Fairy tales and script drama analysis. *Transactional Analysis Bulletin, 7*(26),
39–43. A heuristic, not a measured construct. Note the clash with real victimisation: being
abused is not "taking the victim position", and the workspace never uses Karpman language to
describe someone who was harmed.

## Abuse-specific constructs

### DARVO — Tier A

Deny, Attack, and Reverse Victim and Offender: a response to being held accountable.
Source: Freyd, J. J. (1997). *Feminism & Psychology, 7*(1), 22–32. Measured with the
DARVO questionnaire; perpetrators who were confronted commonly used it, and exposure was
linked with victim self-blame (Harsey, S. J., Zurbriggen, E. L., & Freyd, J. J. (2017).
Perpetrator responses to victim confrontation: DARVO and victim self-blame. *Journal of
Aggression, Maltreatment & Trauma, 26*(6), 644–663). Record each instance with what was
raised and what the response was, step by step.

### Betrayal trauma — Tier A/B

Harm by someone the victim depends on is processed differently, including reduced
awareness of the betrayal, because awareness threatens a needed attachment. Source: Freyd,
J. J. (1996). *Betrayal Trauma: The Logic of Forgetting Childhood Abuse*. Harvard
University Press. Relevant when the user notices they "didn't see it" for years.

### Gaslighting — Tier B

Manipulation that makes the target doubt their perception, memory or sanity, drawing on
power differences. Source: Sweet, P. L. (2019). The sociology of gaslighting. *American
Sociological Review, 84*(5), 851–875. Record the specific claim the person made against
the user's perception, and what the user's own record (sources/) shows.

### Coercive control — Tier B

A pattern of domination through isolation, monitoring, degradation, micro-regulation and
threat, assessed as a pattern rather than as single incidents. Source: Stark, E. (2007).
*Coercive Control: How Men Entrap Women in Personal Life*. Oxford University Press. A
criminal offence in some jurisdictions (for example England and Wales, Serious Crime Act
2015, s. 76). Developed for intimate partners; apply to family relationships as a lens,
labelled as such.

## Outcome and functioning research

### Adverse Childhood Experiences — Tier A (population level)

Ten categories of childhood abuse, neglect and household dysfunction, with dose-response
associations to adult health outcomes. Source: Felitti, V. J., et al. (1998). *American
Journal of Preventive Medicine, 14*(4), 245–258. The original authors warn against using
an individual ACE score to screen or predict for one person (Anda, R. F., Porter, L. E.,
& Brown, D. W. (2020). Inside the adverse childhood experience score: Strengths,
limitations, and misapplications. *American Journal of Preventive Medicine, 59*(2),
293–295). Use the categories to make sure capture does not miss an area; do not compute or
present a score as meaning anything about the user.

### Family functioning models — Tier A

Two validated models give the overview its dimensions:

- **Circumplex model** (Olson, D. H., Sprenkle, D. H., & Russell, C. S. (1979). *Family
  Process, 18*(1), 3–28; measured with FACES): **cohesion** (disengaged ↔ enmeshed),
  **flexibility** (rigid ↔ chaotic) and **communication**.
- **McMaster model** (Epstein, N. B., Baldwin, L. M., & Bishop, D. S. (1983). *Journal of
  Marital and Family Therapy, 9*(2), 171–180; measured with the Family Assessment Device):
  problem solving, communication, roles, affective responsiveness, affective involvement,
  behaviour control.

The workspace does not administer these instruments. It uses their dimensions as headings so
the description of the system is complete and comparable over time.

## Not used here

**Internal Family Systems (IFS)** shares the name but models parts *within* one person, not
a family. If the user works with IFS, their parts work belongs in their own notes or
therapy workspace, not in the actor files.
